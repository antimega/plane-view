Window Seat Forecast

    How the forecast is made

    Routes. Every flight between the same two airports on the sampled days, from adsb.lol's open ADS-B archive. Each past flight counts as one way this flight could go; flights with the same callsign count three times (airlines often fly a number under another callsign, so the airline's usual callsign at that takeoff time stands in), the same airline twice, the same time of day half as much again. Long flights that leave ADS-B coverage over oceans or Siberia are joined along the great circle, and a departure whose arrival fell on a day not in the library is joined to an arrival of the same callsign from another day. With fewer than three flights, departures and arrivals from nearby airports are spliced in. Routes are grouped into variants by runway and path; the map shows each variant's most typical flight.

    What can be seen. Places, peaks, lakes, islands, coasts, rivers and landmarks from GeoNames, Natural Earth and Wikidata along the corridor. From each point of each past flight: distance, direction from the nose, angle below the horizon (with the Earth's curvature and refraction), apparent size and haze. The window's field of view and the ranges at which things are recognisable were calibrated on window photographs from six flights (231 solved camera directions, 967 named features).

    Chance of seeing it. The share of past flights from which it was in view and recognisable, with daylight (at night lit towns, floodlit landmarks and airports count, and parks and water where they make a dark gap in a big city's lights). Sightings in the second half of the flight also give the time before landing, which holds better than the time since takeoff when the cruise runs fast or slow. A clear view is assumed; when the flight is within Open-Meteo's 15-day forecast, the cloud outlook is shown above but does not change the chances. Likely is 55% or more, possible 25–55%, if lucky below.

    From above. Beside each window view is a satellite image of the place from straight above (Sentinel-2 cloudless, 10 m), sized to it and turned to the way you will be looking: you are below the bottom edge, so left and right in the picture are left and right in the window. A town's shape against its coast or river is often what makes it recognisable. The dashed line is the likely route, the white arrow at the bottom is you, and the N marker shows where north is. The route map itself is always north-up.

    The window views. Each sighting has a view rendered from where the aircraft is predicted to be: every pixel is traced to the ground on the Copernicus elevation model (or the sea, with the Earth's curvature), so the skyline is the real one and ridges hide what is behind them, and is then coloured from the satellite picture of that spot (Sentinel-2 cloudless), shaded by the slope, with snow above the season's snowline and a little haze with distance - the same construction as a tilted Google Earth view. The imagery is a cloud-free summer mosaic, so fields and woods will not be in the day's colours, and at night only the towns' lights are drawn. Coastlines, lake shores, rivers and borders are drawn on top, and the sighting is labelled in its side's colour. Times are minutes after takeoff with an estimated clock time; "2 o'clock" counts from the nose, as pilots do.
